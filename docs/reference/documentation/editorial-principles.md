# Editorial principles

These principles guide the voice, character, tone, and structure of the maintainer
documentation. The writing should sound like a thoughtful person explaining
something they understand: direct about the subject, interested in the details
that matter, and considerate of what the reader needs to follow the thought.

Clarity takes more than correct facts and short sentences. A useful document
chooses a point of view, develops its reasoning, and gives its examples enough
context to teach. Its organization should help someone understand the subject
on a first reading and find an answer when they return.

## Voice: speak directly

### Say who does what

A sentence is easier to follow when its subject does something recognizable.
When describing a review, “Willie approves the final text” tells the reader who
makes the decision. “Final approval is required prior to publication” leaves
that person unnamed. The second sentence sounds official, but gives the reader
less to work with.

Prefer active constructions when responsibility matters. Passive voice has its
place when the actor is unknown or irrelevant; the problem is hiding an actor
the reader needs. Address instructions to “you,” and name another person when
the action belongs to them. Do not invent a collective “we” to give the writing
institutional weight.

### Choose words for their meaning

Use familiar words where they express the thought accurately. “Use,” “start,”
and “help” usually carry their meaning more directly than “utilize,” “initiate,”
and “facilitate.” Longer words are appropriate when they name something more
precisely, not when they merely make the sentence sound more important.

Technical terms belong where the subject requires them. Explain an unfamiliar
term when it first matters, then use it consistently. Do not replace a precise
name with a string of vague alternatives to avoid repetition: the reader should
not have to decide whether three phrases refer to one thing.

Exactness also means choosing the right strength of claim. “This prevents the
error” and “This makes the error less likely” promise different results. Choose
the sentence the evidence supports, even when the weaker claim sounds less
impressive.

### Let the sentences carry a thought

Direct writing can still have rhythm and room to breathe. Use a short sentence
for a conclusion or a change of direction. Give a more involved relationship
enough space to unfold. A page made entirely of clipped instructions can feel
impatient and leave the reader to supply the connections between them.

Read a paragraph aloud. Listen for where one sentence prepares the next and
where the writing suddenly changes subject. Words such as “because,” “when,”
and “although” are useful when they make the relationship explicit. Removing
every connective phrase may shorten the prose while making the argument harder
to follow.

### Begin with something the reader can use

A guide about reviewing an article can open with “Check the names, dates, and
quotations before sending the article for approval.” Opening with “This guide
provides an overview of the article review process” announces the document
without beginning its work.

The useful opening depends on the page. A procedure can name the result; an
explanation can introduce the question or tension it will develop. Give the
reader a reason to continue before describing the document's organization.

## Character: let the site come through

### Have a point of view

These docs belong to a personal website shaped by Willie's interests and
judgment. Their character should come from attention to the work: what is worth
preserving, which compromises matter, and why one approach was chosen. A
confident opinion becomes useful when the writing gives the reader enough
reasoning to examine it.

Avoid borrowing the voice of a company announcement. Phrases such as “we are
committed to excellence” claim an attitude without showing any judgment.
Explaining what was chosen, what it improves, and what it costs gives the prose
something particular to say. The reader can understand the choice even when
they would have made a different one.

### Show interest through what you notice

Specific observation carries more character than added enthusiasm. In a
passage about a photograph, noticing how it records the people involved tells
us why it belongs. Calling it “a stunning visual” tells us only that the writer
wants us to admire it.

Choose details that sharpen the subject. A small distinction, a revealing
example, or an unexpected consequence can make an explanation memorable.
Details included only to prove familiarity with the project distract from the
thought. The reader should understand why this detail appears here, not merely
recognize that it is real.

### Leave room for warmth and humor

Warmth comes from taking the reader's time and questions seriously. It can show
up in a patient explanation, an honest acknowledgment of an awkward step, or a
sentence that sounds like a person who cares about the result. It does not
require a greeting, an exclamation mark, or praise for completing an ordinary
action.

Humor works best when it grows out of the subject and remains optional to
understanding it. A dry aside may suit an explanation; a joke in an error
recovery instruction can make a frustrating situation harder to navigate.
Never make the reader's confusion the joke.

Character should survive a plain reading. If removing the playful phrases
leaves nothing distinctive, develop the observations and reasoning rather than
adding more personality markers.

## Tone: treat the reader as a capable peer

### Explain without condescending

A reader can be experienced and still lack the local knowledge a task requires.
Supply that knowledge without treating its absence as a failure. Words such as
“obviously” and “just” often imply that the reader should already understand a
step, even when the explanation has left something out.

Consider an instruction to “just get approval.” It may leave the reader unsure
whose approval is needed, what they should send, or whether revisions require
another review. Name those conditions. The resulting passage can be longer
while being more respectful: it gives the reader information instead of asking
them to guess.

Assume only the knowledge the page's prerequisites establish. Explain the part
that is unfamiliar here, and link to a fuller introduction when necessary.
Neither a lecture on the basics nor an unexplained shorthand helps someone
work independently.

### Match the emotional weight of the situation

Keep ordinary instructions matter-of-fact. Give consequential choices a clear
account of their effects. If an action cannot be reversed, say that before the
reader takes it; dramatic language does not make the consequence more precise.

When something goes wrong, acknowledge the condition and offer a useful next
step. “Check your work” can sound like blame while identifying nothing to check.
“If the name differs between the heading and caption, confirm the spelling with
the source” identifies both the discrepancy and a way to resolve it. A calm
explanation helps the reader regain their footing.

### Distinguish rules, advice, and judgment

Use “must” for a requirement and “should” for a recommendation. Explain where a
requirement comes from when the reader needs that context. A rule imposed by a
publication policy and a suggestion about sentence length have different force;
writing both as commands obscures that distinction.

Editorial judgment rarely becomes useful through an absolute alone. “Keep every
paragraph short” offers an easy measure at the cost of the thought. “Start a new
paragraph when the subject or direction changes” gives the writer a reason to
choose a boundary. Recommendations should help someone exercise judgment, not
replace it with a ritual.

### Be candid about what is known

Distinguish an observation from an interpretation and a proposal from a settled
decision. If a review covered only part of the material, say which part. “The
examples in the opening section were checked” tells the reader more than
“Everything looks good,” and does not borrow certainty from work that was never
done.

Dates matter when a claim can become stale. Keep them beside the observation,
along with the limits needed to interpret it. Candor is not a requirement to
qualify every sentence: state established facts plainly, and preserve
uncertainty where it changes what the reader can conclude.

## Structure: follow the reader's work

### Give each page a primary job

Someone learning a process for the first time needs a path they can follow.
Someone returning to look up a rule needs a place they can find quickly. Choose
which need the page primarily serves before deciding its order and headings.

The [documentation standard](organization.md) distinguishes Tutorials, Guides,
Reference, and Concepts. These categories support different kinds of reading.
They do not require stripping explanation from a procedure: keep the reasoning
that informs the reader's next choice beside that choice, and link to a longer
account when the subject needs one.

### Make the hierarchy express relationships

Major headings should divide the page into substantial subjects or stages.
Subheadings should expose the questions or distinctions within them. In a guide
to reviewing an article, “Check the facts” might contain “Confirm names and
dates” and “Check quotations against their sources.” The hierarchy shows how
those checks contribute to the larger task.

Use H2 for the major divisions, H3 for their useful subdivisions, and H4 when a
subdivision needs another level. Depth should follow the material. A heading
over every incidental distinction fragments the page; one broad heading over
several unrelated decisions conceals its structure.

Prefer headings that name the subject or action. Repeated labels such as
“Overview,” “Details,” and “Notes” reveal little when skimmed. Read the headings
on their own: they should give a returning reader a useful map without requiring
that person to remember the prose beneath them.

### Develop each section on its own terms

Draft each section around the question it needs to answer, then join the sections
and edit their transitions. Do not force every principle into a definition,
three bullets, and a closing slogan. An explanation may need a sustained
argument; a comparison may need a table; a small distinction may be clearest in
one paragraph.

Depth comes from developing the connection between a choice and its consequence.
A rule to verify quotations becomes more useful when the explanation shows what
can go wrong: an accurate sentence taken out of context may misrepresent the
speaker. That reason helps an editor decide what to check beyond the quoted
words themselves.

Let paragraph boundaries follow the thought. Keep an explanation together while
it develops one idea, and begin another paragraph when the direction changes.
A brief paragraph can give a conclusion weight. Several equally sized paragraphs
can flatten an argument if their boundaries follow a visual pattern rather than
the reasoning.

Remove repetition after the argument works. Do not remove the explanation that
makes a rule intelligible merely to make the section look concise.

### Give examples a clear purpose and enough context

Introduce an example through the writing problem it illustrates. If the subject
is a vague instruction, establish what the reader is trying to do, show the
instruction, and explain what it leaves unanswered. The example should make the
editorial choice easier to see without asking the reader to learn an unrelated
system first.

A sentence comparison may need only a short setup. A longer example may need its
audience, situation, and intended result. Include enough to make the example
understandable, then discuss the feature that matters. Extra background can bury
the same point that missing context leaves obscure.

Choose examples for what they teach. A real project detail is useful when its
particulars illuminate the principle. Familiar subject matter is often better
when the lesson concerns voice, tone, or sentence structure. Accuracy alone does
not make an example relevant.

### Keep instructions in the order they become useful

Establish the starting state before the first action. Put a condition before the
step it controls, and a consequence before the decision it affects. Readers
following a procedure should not have to discover halfway through that an
earlier choice needed different preparation.

Show the result at points where the reader needs to decide whether to continue.
A task can involve several stages of completion; identify the outcome each step
establishes. Include recovery and cleanup when the task can leave work behind.
Those details belong to the procedure, not to a vague closing assurance that
things should now work.

### Review the whole after joining the parts

Read the page continuously to check the argument. Look for missing connections,
duplicated introductions, unexplained terms, and conclusions stronger than their
support. A section that worked on its own may need a different opening once the
previous section supplies its context.

Then skim it as someone returning with a question. Check whether the headings
lead to the answer, whether examples make sense where they appear, and whether
a reader can distinguish a rule from its illustration. Both readings matter:
the document needs to develop understanding and remain useful afterward.
